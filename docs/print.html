Introduction

As a university student, you spend many hours studying. Yet, many students are surprised and disappointed by their results. A common reason is that they rely on strategies that feel effective but are not: rereading a textbook chapter, for example, creates a sense of familiarity that is easily mistaken for real knowledge. Cognitive research has identified study techniques that produce more durable learning, yet students tend to underuse them. Self-report studies show that students favour rereading and highlighting over the methods that research supports [@miyatsu2018]. Some reviews have responded by suggesting how students might improve the strategies they already prefer [@miyatsu2018]; a more direct (one might even say, reasonable) route, however, is to adopt the strategies with the strongest evidence. This essay therefore asks how university students can optimize their learning, and answers by discussing three evidence-based strategies: pretesting, retrieval practice, and spaced practice. Effective studying is not simply a matter of effort, but of method.

Pretesting

The first strategy is pretesting: attempting to answer questions about material before studying it, even if the answers to the questions must be guessed. Although this seems counterintuitive, research indicates that answering questions about a topic before reading about it improves later recall [@putnam2016]. The benefit arises because the attempt activates any prior knowledge and prepares the reader to connect new information with what is already known. Even incorrect guesses are useful, because they make the reader notice and remember the correct information when it appears. Prequizzing is becoming easier to practice. [@putnam2016] suggests that before a studying session (like a reading session or a lecture), a student can write down a few questions about the topic and attempt to answer them, then return to those questions afterwards to check and correct the answers. However, many textbooks also include postreading quizzed. They can also be easily repurposed for pretesting. Thirdly, the rise of AI gives us new opportunities. A chatbot can create a quiz for you after reading your lecture slides or textbook chapter. It is even possible to prompt it to follow your precise specified needs such as difficulty level. The main obstacle is psychological rather than practical: guessing at material one has not yet studied feels wrong, and students generally prefer to read first. Recognising that pretesting is a low-cost way of preparing the memory, rather than a test of current knowledge, helps to overcome this hesitation.

Reflection

Writing this essay was more instructive than I expected. The two articles described study strategies that I have rarely used, and one in particular stood out: the read–recite–review method. Reading about it, I realised that I had planned to recite and review the articles after reading them, but never did, and I am now fairly sure that this is why I remember so little of their detail. The exercise also made me notice how often I choose study methods because they feel productive rather than because they work.
